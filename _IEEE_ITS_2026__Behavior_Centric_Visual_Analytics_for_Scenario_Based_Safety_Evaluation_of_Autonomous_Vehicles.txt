Behavior-Centric Visual Analytics for Scenario-Based
Safety Evaluation of Autonomous Vehicles
Shih-Yi Chiu, Wen-Chieh Lin, Yu-Shuen Wang, Chen-Hui Hu, Chieh-Chih Wang

Abstract—Evaluating the safety of autonomous vehicles (AVs)
requires testing across diverse conditions to characterize their
Operational Design Domain (ODD) and its boundaries. Scenariobased testing has emerged as a key approach, yet interpreting
its results remains challenging due to the complexity and scale
of simulation data. We present a visual analytics framework for
behavior-centric analysis of scenario-based AV testing, linking AV
behaviors with scenario parameters (e.g., oncoming vehicle speed
and start delay) to support interpretable safety assessment. By
integrating dimensionality reduction, clustering, and coordinated
visualizations, the framework reveals diverse interaction patterns
and identifies safety-critical regions, such as collision boundaries and regions of high risk variation. We demonstrate the
framework through three case studies—intersection navigation,
overtaking, and multi-lane cut-ins—which illustrate its ability
to uncover nuanced behavioral differences, analyze boundary
conditions, and compare AV planning systems. Expert feedback
indicates that the framework provides interpretable and actionable insights to support safety-oriented behavioral analysis in
simulation-based AV testing workflows.
Index Terms—Methods for safety, Data analytics and data
science, Autonomous driving, Operational design domain

I. I NTRODUCTION
Autonomous vehicles (AVs) must demonstrate safe operation across diverse conditions within their Operational Design
Domain (ODD) [1]. Scenario-based testing provides an effective approach to evaluate safety performance [2], [3] by defining a scenario parameter space that captures traffic situations
through variable parameters (e.g., oncoming vehicle speed,
start delay) [4]. Each point in this space instantiates a concrete
scenario (hereafter, a scenario), corresponding to a specific test
execution. Systematically varying these parameters enables the
identification of safe, failure, and boundary scenarios using
collision outcomes [3] or criticality metrics [5]. This paradigm
supports coverage-driven testing of the ODD [6] and facilitates
characterization of safety-critical boundaries [7]. Recent work
further explores generating diverse and critical scenarios to
improve test coverage and efficiency [6]–[12].
Despite these advances, interpreting results from scenariobased testing remains challenging. Prior studies primarily
report aggregated safety outcomes, such as criticality metrics

Visual analytics offers a promising approach to transform
raw simulation data into interpretable representations. It supports understanding of AV behavior, anomaly detection, and
safety analysis, helping stakeholders build trust in AV systems [13]. While automated behavior labeling could categorize

the nuanced dynamics of multi-agent behavior. By keeping

analyzing interaction trajectories provides richer insight into
how AVs respond to dynamic agents and varying scenario

In this work, we present a visual analytics framework for
behavior-centric analysis of scenario-based AV testing. The
framework characterizes interaction trajectories, clusters recurrent behavioral patterns, and links these behaviors to scenario
parameters and safety outcomes. It integrates coordinated,
linked views that support filtering, comparison, and drill-down
across multiple perspectives, enabling efficient exploration and
deeper interpretation of AV behavior.
We demonstrate the framework on the safety evaluation
of the AV’s motion planning module, where vehicle actions
and agent interactions are directly observable in trajectory
data. Three case studies, including intersection navigation,
overtaking, and multi-lane cut-ins, show how the framework
reveals distinct behavioral patterns near safety-critical regions,
such as collision boundaries and regions of high risk variation.
These results suggest that the approach supports comparative
evaluation of planning modules and highlights the importance
of behavior-level analysis in safety validation.
This paper contributes an interpretable, ODD-aware visual
analytics workflow for AV safety assessment that supports systematic exploration across large scenario spaces. The proposed
system enables visual examination of behavioral and safety
variations over scenario parameters, including conditions both
within and beyond the ODD. By integrating behavior-centric
representations with interactive analysis, the workflow exposes
recurring patterns and behavioral differences across thousands
of simulated cases, facilitating more nuanced interpretation of
AV behavior. More broadly, this work highlights the value of
visual analytics in complementing existing automated testing
pipelines, supporting more informed evaluation and decision-

C. Visualizations in Safety-Critical Scenario Exploration

A. Vehicle Trajectory Visualization
Vehicle trajectory visualization provides insights into how
vehicles move through space and time. Classical methods,
such as space–time cubes, stacked trajectories, and density heatmaps, reveal motion structures and traffic flow patterns [14], [15]. However, these approaches are primarily
designed for macroscopic transportation analysis, focusing
on aggregate trends rather than safety-critical behaviors or
scenario-level reasoning in AV testing. More recent work
moves toward AV-oriented analysis by introducing visualization and data management frameworks for large-scale driving
data, supporting querying, organization, and visual exploration
for scenario-based evaluation [16], [17].
Despite these advances, visualization of AV interaction
behaviors within scenario parameter spaces remains limited.
Existing replay-based or re-simulation tools operate at the
level of individual runs, allowing users to replay or modify
an instantiated scenario to examine system responses under
varying conditions [18]–[21]. As the number of variations
increases, however, analysts must inspect many runs individually, making the process time-consuming and visually
cluttered. This workflow hinders systematic analysis, limiting
the ability to compare behaviors across variations, identify
consistent patterns, and extract higher-level insights.
B. Scenario Space Exploration for AV Safety Assessment
AV safety evaluation is grounded in system performance
across diverse conditions defined by the ODD. To enable
systematic evaluation, researchers adopt parametric scenario
testing [4], [6], where scenario variables (e.g., agent behaviors)
are represented within a formal parameter space. Safety validation is then formulated as a search problem over this space to
identify boundary conditions where system performance fails
to meet the safety requirements.
Various methods have been proposed to efficiently explore scenario parameter spaces and identify safety-critical
regions, including adaptive sampling [8], importance sampling [22], and surrogate modeling approaches such as neural
networks [9], probabilistic metamodels [10], and Gaussian
process regression [11]. These techniques aim to characterize
safety boundaries in high-dimensional spaces by estimating
regions associated with failure or degraded performance. More
recent work [12] further incorporates agent behaviors into
the exploration process, enabling coordinated searches across
scenario and behavior spaces to discover behaviorally diverse
and safety-critical cases.
While effective for identifying safety-critical regions, these
approaches often produce large collections of trajectories that
are difficult to interpret collectively. Existing methods emphasize the discovery of safety-critical regions but provide limited

Most scenario-space exploration methods [6]–[12] are not
designed as visualization techniques; their primary goal is
to identify safety-critical regions efficiently. Visualization is
typically used as a secondary tool to present results, such as
outcome fields or safety boundaries.
For instance, UNECE R157 [3] visualizes collision outcomes from parameter sweeps for automated lane keeping
system certification. Similarly, surrogate models [9], [11]
interpolate safety outcomes over parameter spaces, enabling
visualization of outcome fields or decision boundaries for
diagnostic analysis or benchmarking across scenarios.
However, these visualizations focus on where safety-critical
boundaries occur rather than how AV behaviors evolve as
scenario parameters vary. They emphasize final outcomes
without capturing the behavioral variations leading to them.
Although prior work has visualized AV decision-making logic
for failure analysis or causal interpretation [23]–[25], these
approaches typically focus on individual scenarios and do not
provide a systematic view of behavior variation across the
parameter space.
D. Research Gaps
Based on the literature, we identify four key gaps (R1–R4)
in the visualization and analysis of AV behavior within scenario parameter spaces. Existing approaches provide limited
support in the following aspects:
• R1. Scalable behavior summarization. Existing replaybased tools lack efficient mechanisms to aggregate and
summarize AV behaviors across large numbers of scenarios
in parameter space, limiting scalability in analysis.
• R2. Understanding parameter-driven behavioral variations. Prior work rarely reveals how behaviors vary with
underlying scenario parameters, making it difficult to identify triggers of behavioral changes and how variations arise.
• R3. Integrating behavior with safety context. Existing
visualizations identify safe and unsafe regions but rarely
incorporate the behavioral variations that lead to these
outcomes, particularly near ODD boundaries.
• R4. Multi-scale behavioral analysis. Existing approaches
do not effectively support seamless exploration across analysis scales, making it difficult to relate trajectory-level
behaviors in individual scenarios to aggregated patterns
across the scenario parameter space, or to compare behavior
patterns across AV systems.

This section presents our visual analytics framework, including its design goals and the methodologies and implementations of its components.

A. Design Goals
Based on the research gaps (R1–R4) identified in Section II,
we define four design goals (G1–G4) to guide the design of
our visualization framework.
G1. Scalable and interpretable analysis of AV behaviors.
To address the challenge of summarizing AV behaviors
across the scenario parameter space (R1), the system should
automatically cluster trajectories and assist in summarizing
recurring behavioral patterns from large-scale data. These
summaries should be presented through interpretable visual
forms that reveal trajectory structures and behavioral differences, enabling systematic analysis beyond single-scenario
inspection.
• G2. Linking behaviors to scenario parameters. To address the limited connection between behaviors and scenario
parameters (R2), the system should integrate behavioral
summaries with parameter space representations. By mapping trajectory clusters to regions of the parameter space,
the system reveals how parameter variations correspond to
distinct behavioral responses.
• G3. Behavior-aware safety-related region analysis. To integrate behavioral analysis with safety context (R3), the system should combine behavioral representations with safety
outcomes, enabling analysis of how behaviors vary across
safe and unsafe regions and how these variations influence
safety outcomes. This supports diagnostic reasoning near
safety-critical boundaries and regions of high risk variation.
• G4. Multi-scale and Interactive Exploration. To address
the lack of cross-scale analysis (R4), the system should
support seamless exploration across scales, from trajectorylevel behaviors in individual scenarios to aggregated patterns across the scenario parameter space. Through interactive, cross-scale linking, users can relate local behaviors
to global patterns for safety analysis, and compare behavioral patterns across scenarios, including those near critical
regions and boundaries, as well as across AV systems.

B. System Overview
Fig. 1 presents an overview of the system for exploring AV
interaction trajectories within a scenario parameter space. The
system integrates trajectory clustering, synchronized replay,
trajectory heatmaps, and scenario-parameter visualizations, all
coordinated through linked views with interactive filtering and
selection. These components support comparison across scenarios and focused examination of behavioral patterns under
varying parameter settings.
This exploration is organized as a multi-stage workflow
(Fig. 2) that guides users from raw simulation data to interpretable insights. The process begins by projecting and clustering trajectory data to produce low-dimensional representations
and candidate clusterings that capture potential behavioral
patterns.
Users then preview and select candidate clustering results
through the clustering selection view and projection view. With
a selected clustering, they use the heatmaps and replayer to
understand behavioral differences across clusters, and examine

3

how clusters distribute over the scenario parameter space, revealing how behavioral patterns relate to parameter variations
and safety outcomes. Users can further focus on safety-critical
boundaries or regions of high risk variation by filtering these
areas and exploring them in detail through coordinated views.
Through this workflow, users move from raw data to interpretable insights. By integrating trajectory clustering with
interactive visualization, the system helps users compare
clusters and their behavioral differences, uncover unexpected
behaviors, understand how scenario parameters shape those
behaviors, and examine how safety-related risks emerge, ultimately supporting the development of safer and more reliable
AVs. The following sections describe the underlying methods
of each visual analysis component.
C. Behavioral Analysis: Trajectory Clustering and Projection
To identify recurring behavioral patterns (G1) within a
scenario parameter space, the system analyzes interaction
trajectories generated under each scenario. These trajectories
are high-dimensional, multivariate time series capturing spatiotemporal dynamics (e.g., position, speed, and direction) across
multiple vehicles and numerous scenarios. Users can choose
which attributes to include, allowing the analysis to adapt
to different traffic contexts (e.g., road structures and traffic
conditions) or specific safety requirements. However, directly
analyzing raw trajectories at scale is computationally prohibitive, motivating compact representations that preserve key
interaction characteristics while enabling efficient clustering
for behavioral pattern discovery.
We adopt Multivariate Functional Principal Component
Analysis (MFPCA) [26] to derive low-dimensional representations of trajectories. MFPCA models each trajectory as a multivariate function over time and summarizes it using Functional
Principal Component (FPC) scores, capturing dominant modes
of variation. This yields a unified embedding suitable for
clustering and visualization. Compared to deep learning–based
embeddings (e.g., autoencoder) that require training and hyperparameter tuning, MFPCA offers a more transparent and
computationally efficient alternative for exploratory analysis.
MFPCA represents each trajectory over the time domain
using population-level mean and covariance functions (Appendix A). We retain components explaining over 95% of the
variance, and use the resulting FPC scores for clustering and
projection.
To handle variable-length trajectories caused by eventdriven termination (e.g., collisions), we adopt a padding strategy that preserves termination time information while enabling
functional analysis. Specifically, early-terminated trajectories
are padded with their own mean values plus low-level Gaussian noise [27]–[29]. This provides a neutral continuation
without introducing population-level artifacts. Unlike distancebased methods such as Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) [30],
which rely on pairwise alignment and do not produce explicit
embeddings, this approach supports efficient, scalable clustering.

D. Replayer
To support intuitive comparison of behavioral differences
across scenarios, the system includes a replayer (Fig. 1(e))
that synchronizes trajectory playback along a shared timeline
(G1, G4). Scenarios from different clusters are displayed
in separate windows, enabling side-by-side comparison to
distinguish cluster-level behavioral differences.
The replayer presents a top-down 2D map with animated
trajectories of the AV and surrounding agents, effectively
replaying the simulation. By aligning and showing multiple

scenarios on a common timeline, it enables direct comparison
of behavioral differences both within and across clusters.
Interactive timeline controls (e.g., time slider, play/pause)
allow users to inspect specific moments in detail. Depending
on filtering or user selection (e.g., lasso or clicking), the
replayer adapts to display either a single scenario or a group
of scenarios. When multiple scenarios are selected, vehicles
from all scenarios are overlaid at the same timestamp, enabling
direct spatial comparison across scenarios and clusters. When
a single scenario is selected, the replayer presents its detailed
temporal evolution. This design supports both comparison of
behavioral patterns and variations across scenarios and clusters
and detailed inspection of individual cases, enabling flexible,
multi-scale visual analysis (G4).

scenarios in a compact, aligned representation (G1). Specifically, it displays trajectory features (e.g., AV speed, acceleration), with one heatmap per feature and cluster, and allows
users to choose which features to visualize.

To enable meaningful cross-scenario comparison, trajectories are aligned to a shared reference, either temporal or
spatial. Temporal alignment highlights the evolution of behaviors (e.g., braking or acceleration), while spatial alignment
aligns trajectories by road location, enabling comparison at the
same position despite speed differences. These complementary
modes provide both temporal and spatial perspectives for
analysis.
Specifically, the heatmap supports two alignment modes:
time step and road s-distance, where s denotes longitudinal
distance along the road centerline. In time-step mode, columns
correspond to trajectory states sampled at 0.1-second intervals.
In s-distance mode, columns align trajectories at fixed spatial
intervals (1/4 meter). Feature values between samples are interpolated. These modes enable direct comparison of behaviors
at corresponding temporal or spatial locations across scenarios.
The integration with the replayer differs by alignment mode.
In time-step mode, a gray indicator moves across the heatmap
to mark the current replay time, linking animation to temporal
segments. In s-distance mode, the indicator represents the
ego vehicle’s longitudinal position, and time controls are
replaced by an s-distance slider, allowing comparison of agent
configurations when the AV reaches the same road location
across scenarios.

F. Scenario Parameter Space
The scenario parameter space view (Fig. 1(c), Fig. 3)
visualizes scenarios within a two-dimensional parameter space
to link behavioral patterns and safety outcomes with scenario
parameters (G2 & G3). The x- and y-axes correspond to
selected scenario parameters. Each scenario is represented as
a point, where color encodes its trajectory cluster and shape
indicates the collision outcome (cross for collision, dot for
non-collision), enabling identification of safety outcomes such
as collision boundaries (or ODD) across the parameter space.
Building on this parameter–behavior–safety mapping,
the view further supports risk analysis by incorporating criticality metrics (G3). A background layer can be overlaid by
interpolating either criticality values or their gradient magnitudes, forming a continuous risk field. The color encoding
reflects either risk levels or the magnitude of risk variation
(i.e., gradient magnitude). Users can toggle foreground and
background layers to support focused exploration, as illustrated in Fig. 3.
Together, these visual encodings enable the identification
of salient regions in the parameter space, including cluster
transitions, collision boundaries, and regions of rapid risk
variation, supporting interpretation of scenario-based results
and safety/risk analysis for improving AV system robustness.

regions, criticality gradient magnitude, or custom selections
made in any view. Boundary scenarios are identified using a
k-nearest-neighbor criterion in the parameter space: a scenario
is considered a boundary one if its k nearest neighbors include
different collision outcomes (collision boundary) or different

D. Expert Review and Feedback

and rated their task confidence. Participants then engaged in
an open exploration phase using a think-aloud protocol. After
completing both phases, they provided quantitative feedback
through the System Usability Scale (SUS) and a customized
domain-specific effectiveness questionnaire, followed by semistructured interviews. Detailed protocols and survey instruments are provided in Appendix E.

Expert 1 described the heatmap as “impressively useful” for
“quickly identifying behavioral differences,” while Expert 4
noted that synchronized cross-view interactions made analysis
“intuitive” (G4). Experts also identified previously overlooked
safety issues, including inconsistent yielding behavior and
potential rear-end collision risks (G3). These findings suggest
that the framework effectively supports safety-oriented analysis in simulation-based AV testing workflows.

VI. C ONCLUSION
This work presents a novel visual analytics framework
for scenario-based testing of autonomous vehicles, addressing
the limitations of outcome-focused methods by emphasizing
behavior-centric analysis. Through clustering and interactive
exploration of trajectory data, our system enables stakeholders
to identify distinct trajectory patterns and behavioral differences, relate them to scenario parameters, and uncover
how these differences correspond to regions where safety
performance degrades. The case studies demonstrate that the
framework provides insight into how AV behavior affects
safety across scenarios and enables a deeper understanding of
safety-performance differences between AV planning modules
through behavior-based comparisons.
Expert feedback highlights the framework’s ability to generate interpretable and actionable insights, particularly in exposing safety-related behavioral differences and guiding decisions
about system adjustments. Ultimately, this behavior-focused
approach contributes to building safer and more reliable autonomous vehicle systems by bridging the gap between raw
simulation data and interpretable safety analysis.

A PPENDIX D
C ASE S TUDY 3: A NALYSIS OF S AFETY C ONFLICT IN
P OST-PASS B RAKING
To investigate the unexpected braking behavior observed in
braking-rear-end (C4) and pass-slowdown (C6), we consulted
with the AV developers. As shown in Fig. 15(b), both clusters

initially resemble a successful overtaking maneuver similar to
pass-first (C1). However, the heatmaps reveal a critical safety
conflict in the post-pass phase: the ego acceleration profiles for
C4 and C6 show a distinct braking region (red) immediately
after passing (indicated by the gray marker), whereas C1
maintains a more consistent profile.
The developers explained that this braking is a reactive
safety response to avoid a potential collision with a static
obstacle (the parked vehicle). When braking occurs earlier
(C6), the following vehicle (i.e., the cut-in vehicle) has sufficient time to decelerate. In contrast, when braking occurs
later—immediately after passing—it reduces the available
reaction time for the following vehicle, increasing the risk of
rear-end collisions (C4). The abrupt deceleration in C4 is thus
a key factor contributing to this risk.
Further analysis, guided by the developers, indicates that
this behavior is triggered by a collision-checking mechanism based on the ego vehicle’s projected right boundary.
Specifically, the system evaluates whether the ego vehicle’s
right edge, extended forward, would intersect with the parked
vehicle. As illustrated in Fig. 15(c), the projected boundary
(black line) in C4 and C6 intersects with the parked vehicle
downstream, prompting braking. In contrast, in C1, sufficient
lateral clearance is maintained, and no intersection occurs.
These findings illustrate how the proposed visual analytics
framework supports collaborative diagnosis with AV developers, enabling the identification of behavior-level conflicts
and potential limitations in planning strategies. In this case, a
possible mitigation—e.g., increasing lateral clearance from the
parked vehicle—may in turn reduce clearance from the cutin vehicle during overtaking, highlighting the complexity of
planning under competing spatial constraints. This example
demonstrates how the system helps reveal such intertwined
considerations and supports informed refinement of planning
behaviors.
